Thyroid Cancer: Understanding the Four Main Types, How They Spread, and Their Prognosis

Written by Dr. Adlina | Published by BA Medicale

Thyroid carcinoma represents the most common malignancy of the endocrine system [1]. Based on cellular origin, histopathological features, and biological behavior, thyroid carcinomas are broadly categorized into four primary histological subtypes: papillary, follicular, medullary, and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma [1,2].

Understanding these differences is essential because each type has distinct patterns of progression, metastatic behaviour, and prognosis.

Key Highlights

• Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC): The most common type, accounting for nearly 90% of cases, with generally excellent long-term survival.

• Follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC): The second most common type, with a greater tendency to spread

through the bloodstream.

• Medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC): A rare neuroendocrine cancer, approximately 25% of cases being

hereditary.

• Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC): A rare but extremely aggressive cancer associated with poor

survival outcomes.

1. Papillary Thyroid Carcinoma (PTC) The Most Common Type, With an Excellent Prognosis

Prevalence and cellular origin

Papillary thyroid carcinoma accounts for nearly 90% of all thyroid cancer cases [1]. It originates from

thyroid follicular cells and is most commonly diagnosed between the ages of 30 and 50.

Women are approximately three times more likely than men to develop PTC [2].

How it spreads

PTC primarily spreads through the lymphatic system to lymph nodes in the neck. Approximately 70% of

patients may develop cervical lymph node metastases[2,3]. Distant metastases are relatively uncommon [2].

Prognosis

PTC generally has an excellent prognosis, with 10-year survival rates exceeding 90%.

In selected cases diagnosed at an early stage, cure rates may approach 100% [1,3].

However, individual outcomes depend on disease stage and other clinical factors.

2. Follicular Thyroid Carcinoma (FTC) A Thyroid Cancer That Can Spread Through the Bloodstream

Prevalence and cellular origin

Follicular thyroid carcinoma is the second most common thyroid cancer, accounting for approximately

10–20% of cases.

Its reported frequency may increase to 25–40% in iodine-deficient endemic regions [1].

FTC originates from thyroid follicular cells and is generally diagnosed at an older age (average ~60 years)

How it spreads

Unlike papillary thyroid carcinoma, FTC tends to spread through the bloodstream, a process known as

hematogenous metastasis.

Common distant metastatic sites include the lungs and bones (approximately 6–20% of cases), while lymph

node involvement is relatively uncommon, affecting fewer than 10% [3].

Prognosis

FTC generally has a favorable prognosis, with approximately 80% of cases having relatively indolent behaviour.

However, FTC can be more aggressive than PTC because of its tendency to spread through the

bloodstream [1,3].

Long-term outcomes depend on the extent of disease and the presence of distant metastases.

3. Medullary Thyroid Carcinoma (MTC) A Rare Thyroid Cancer With a Genetic Connection

Prevalence and cellular origin

Medullary thyroid carcinoma is a rare neuroendocrine malignancy, accounting for approximately 1–5% of

thyroid cancers [4].

Unlike papillary and follicular thyroid cancers, MTC originates from parafollicular C cells, which produce

the hormone calcitonin [1].

Hereditary characteristics

Approximately 75% of MTC cases occur sporadically, without an inherited familial pattern.

The remaining 25% are hereditary and may be associated with:

• Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 2A (MEN2A)

• Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia type 2B (MEN2B)

• Familial Medullary Thyroid Cancer (FMTC)

These hereditary conditions are clinically important because genetic susceptibility can affect family

members [1,5].

Prognosis

Overall survival rate of MTC estimates by stage:

Stage I — 100%

Stage II — 93%

Stage III — 71%

Stage IV — 21%

Treatment outcomes depend heavily on early detection, complete surgical management, and whether

distant metastases are present.

4. Anaplastic Thyroid Carcinoma (ATC) The Rarest but Most Aggressive Major Type

Prevalence and cellular origin

Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma accounts for approximately 1–2% of thyroid cancer cases [1].

It is an undifferentiated, highly aggressive malignancy associated with thyroid follicular cells.

ATC predominantly affects older adults, particularly individuals over 65 years of age [3].

How it spreads

ATC can rapidly invade surrounding structures in the neck and spread to distant organs, including the

lungs, bones, and brain.

Approximately 20–50% of patients already have distant metastases when the disease is first diagnosed

[1].

Prognosis

ATC has a particularly poor prognosis.

Although it represents only a small proportion of thyroid cancers, it is reported that it

accounts for up to 50% of thyroid cancer-related deaths.

Historically reported outcomes include:

• Average survival of approximately 5–6 months following diagnosis.

• Fewer than 20% of patients surviving beyond one year [1].

These historical figures should not be interpreted as predictions for individual patients. Prognosis may

differ with disease characteristics and available treatments.

Key Takeaways A thyroid cancer diagnosis does not automatically mean a poor prognosis.

Most thyroid cancers are papillary or follicular carcinomas, which generally have favorable outcomes,

particularly when detected and managed appropriately.

However, medullary thyroid cancer may require consideration of hereditary risk, while anaplastic thyroid

cancer demands urgent clinical attention because of its aggressive nature.

The specific cancer type, stage, and extent of disease are critical factors in determining prognosis.
